Qualities I Admire or Look For

Nov 2026

There have been plenty of posts about what people look for, whether in founders, high-agency people, or highly successful people in any field. I wanted to write down the qualities I really admire and look for in people. Some of them you’ve probably read about before. Others, maybe not.

Broadly speaking, successful people tend to share a couple of traits. One of them is an obsession with their craft, or an obsession with self-improvement.

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) is part of why AI agents are both exciting and worrying. An agent that can keep improving itself could eventually reach a point where it’s out of control without human oversight. Now apply that to a person. If somebody can, psychologically or in some other way, keep improving and keep getting better, they’re going to be very successful. It’s a matter of time.

So how do you do that? I think there are two ways. Either you’re so obsessed with what you’re doing that you can’t focus on anything else, completely locked in, or you have something that carries you through the parts/periods you’re not obsessed with.

A lot of people focus on the first one: being obsessed with your craft, being super passionate about your work. I definitely think that’s important, and nearly every successful person has it. But there’s an overlooked quality that’s just as important, more the second point, which I call tolerance. That’s the first quality I want to talk about.

1. Tolerance

By tolerance, I mean the ability to tolerate pain, suffering, hardship, or bouts of anxiety, and to have the tenacity to keep working through them. It could mean getting past a hard obstacle. It could also mean doing the boring work you need to do to be successful, enduring consistent boredom or friction to unlock long term growth and reward.

It’s easier to be obsessed with something when you’re really good at it, or when you’ve gone deep enough into it that it starts to genuinely interest you. But before that happens, there’s usually a stretch where you’re not that interested and you put in the work anyway. You sit down and learn it, or you go out and practice it, until you’re either very good at it or very passionate about it.

That passion isn’t guaranteed to come easily. You need tolerance to persevere until it does.

On top of that, if you start out doing badly at something, it can get demoralizing after a while. Defeating, even. But if you keep going and push past that point, you might get through it.

Most people quit when they’re about 80% of the way there, which is usually right when they’re closest. A line often attributed to Ross Perot (founder: electronic data systems, perot systems; angel investor in NeXT) puts this simply: “Most people give up just when they’re about to achieve success.” Pushing through that last stretch counts as tolerance, too.

Here’s an example from my own life. I grew up hating golf. I honestly didn’t like it at all. I signed up for my first tournament in seventh grade and played very badly. I wasn’t used to the pressure, and it was really, really overwhelming. I thought I’d never touch a club again.

But I forced myself, quite literally, to get back on the course and keep playing, because I knew I wanted to play a sport in college. I wanted to be an athlete, but I couldn’t compete in sports like basketball because I wasn’t born with the 7 feet wingspan genetics.

Golf was my best shot at an advantage, because in my opinion it mostly comes down to hard work. There are some genetic advantages, like hand-eye coordination (which I’m fairly good at), but I figured golf gave me the best chance. Even if I played badly, if I kept pushing, I’d get better.

By my junior year of high school, I’d gotten very good. For about two years, I never went more than one day without touching a golf club, practicing for multiple hours every single day.

Somewhere in there, I realized I’d started to really enjoy it. I enjoyed going to the range to hit balls. I enjoyed going out on the course on a windy day to practice. I became genuinely passionate about it. I really wanted to succeed, and I wanted to practice every single day. I itched to get on the course.

But that feeling didn’t come quickly. I had to develop it over time.

During that development phase, you’re not going to enjoy every day of practice. A lot of the time it’ll feel like you’re forcing yourself. The ability to sit through that period is incredibly valuable.

I think that applies beyond childhood, too. People who learn to endure pain and hardship over the long term carry that with them.

Anybody I see with a really strong ability to tolerate pain, boredom, and hardship has a quality I think is really cool. I’ve seen some people who have it, and they’re almost always able to do well.

That’s also why people from disadvantaged backgrounds, who may not have the same resources and opportunities, sometimes end up doing very well for themselves. The same way why most rich, spoiled kids will rarely amount to anything of their own volition; they have a very low tolerance threshold.

2. Gravitas

My tolerance is something I’m personally proud of. I had a lot of hardships growing up, and I’ve done plenty of things where I persevered and it paid off greatly. The second quality is different. It’s one I’m still refining, and I definitely don’t think I’m there yet.

Gravitas.

If you look it up, you’ll find definitions about presence or solemnity of manner: being serious and earnest in how you carry yourself, in a way that earns people’s deep respect.

I define it more broadly as competence plus charisma. It’s not just being charming so that somebody likes you or thinks you’re such a cool guy. It goes deeper than that. When you meet somebody with gravitas, they have a presence that pulls you in, makes you deeply admire them, and makes you take what they’re saying very seriously.

That’s very rare.

Steve Jobs was a good example. He could come into a room with high intensity and agency, ask the most of everybody there, and still command the utmost respect.

You might not like him as a founder, but he could still bring you in to share the same goal. He united people, led people, and was very good at recruiting and marketing. That all comes from gravitas.

Watch any of his product launches, like the iPod or the iPhone, or his recorded interviews over the years, especially the ones in classrooms. The intensity when he speaks and the eye contact he makes are incredibly effective at getting his ideas across.

Gravitas is a very valuable skill, and like charisma, I think you can get better at it. But it takes time. It’s also easy to see through. If you try to fake gravitas, it backfires, and you end up looking kind of cringe.

3. Temperance

The third quality is temperance.

I don’t think you need temperance to be successful. Plenty of successful people have very little of it. I’d actually argue that successful people fail to practice it.

But this list is also about what I admire, and temperance is something I admire deeply and really try to live by.

I’ve had personal experiences where failing to practice temperance led to very negative outcomes. And I see it play out every day, with somebody messing up their life one way or another because they couldn’t practice it.

The idea is simple: do things in moderation.

I’m not saying you can’t be generous, or that you can’t go deep into one subject. I’m talking more about making sure you don’t end up at the extremes.

It’s very simple. Here’s a few examples:

Generous is good. Too generous is pushover or easy to have your time taken advantage of.

Self-prioritization is good. Too much of that becomes selfishness, and this leads to bad.

Another example, a strong desire to earn and keep your own money can make you relentless in pursuing it. It can make you calculated, hard to trick, and good at gaining the advantage in any situation. Take that to the extreme, though, and you get greed. That’s a pretty negative thing to have, because not every relationship or transaction needs to be run as a personal calculation. It just compromises your relationships. Ironically, because this makes you view everything as a zero sum game, and well that can lead to less gain and more loss in the long run.

Look at almost anyone, and they usually fail to practice temperance in one way or another. So the person I admire most is the one who can do a lot of great things while still practicing temperance and staying in control.

The come-back I get hit with the most after saying this is: well what about for things that are really hard to do only in moderation. My answer is this: if something is very hard to do in moderation, just don’t do it at all.

Trying to do it in moderation anyway, telling yourself you’ll only do a little bit, is really hard. You can get too caught up in it, and once you do, you lose sight of whatever benefit it had. You become tied to it. That’s why you have to choose what you do without getting tied down to the extremes.

Cutting something out completely can sound extreme itself. But actually, if you’re not tied to anything, you can get through the day without feeling like you have to do something or be reliant on that. It’s totally fine for periods of time to take out something. For instance, I have deactivated social media for a few months at a time when I need to focus, as “doom scrolling in moderation is harder than just not scrolling period”. [Now I use SocialLite so reels are blocked and I can still keep tabs on friends].

You don’t have unlimited willpower. You have a finite amount every day, and you’d rather spend it making confident decisions at work or on something important than waste it on everyday decisions that don’t matter as much. You will also find as you practice temperance more and more and become more mindful of reliances your judgment is less clouded and your days are more free.

4. Belief

The last quality is what some people call manifestation, or the power to believe in yourself. It’s having overwhelming confidence in your ability to succeed, even in the face of strong opposition.

If you think negatively about yourself, you can feel yourself spiraling. Things look more and more bleak, and the problems keep piling up.

Just recently in my own case, I thought it would be great to check up on any health issues. I wired my brain into looking for issues about my body. If you look for issues, you’ll find them. I compiled a list of “sus” things about my body and found more and more I would feel weird sensations like chest pain or a weird bump on my arm. This ended up making me more paranoid about getting cancer or any sicknesses, and ultimately I realized it’s a net negative to my health. I’m 23, normal checks and physicals say I’m a very healthy boy. I don’t need to be paranoid; just stick to the basics and I should be fine.

If you think positively about yourself and frame things that way, you often find that a lot of things actually work out in your favor. Having the mindset of a winner instead of a victim is a great mental reframe.

Plenty of great athletes credit this. Cristiano Ronaldo has said his belief that no player is better than him is the key to his success. Kobe Bryant talked a lot about visualization and self-belief. Same with Novak Djokovic. I really believe that.

On the medical side, people also point to the placebo effect. There are plenty of medical studies showing that your beliefs and expectations can influence certain outcomes. In my case, stepping out of the micro-stress about my body health actually made me feel a lot better and healthier.

Unfortunately, that works the other way, too. When you believe something negative about yourself, it can become reality.
