A guide on learning

Nov 2026

I’m writing this piece for anyone who is trying to learn better. Learning is a skill you can get better and better at. This piece has some wisdom I have gathered from my learning journey. The failures I had and how I handled learning may be particularly useful to you. This piece best applies to autodidacticism and andragogy, but its principles are based in structure from mentor-led education and inspired from pedagogy.

About 3-4 months into BCG, I wanted to make sure I stayed close to AI startups. I started doing some learning and building in my free time in an effort to not be too removed from the daily happenings and updates in this industry traveling in “post-processable velocity” (term from Jim VandeHei in this Axios article, which I find very well put).

Given the demands of the nature of my work and my personal knack for rest and exercise over the weekends, in practice this really mainly meant occasional vibecoding sessions and 5 min flips through The Information for tech&ai news and LinkedIn for new funds daily. Every once in a while I’d also go down a deep rabbit hole for a few hours on a certain topic, i.e. Attritable UUVs in defense tech startups.

I thought by doing this I would be learning a good deal and upskilling in AI. However, more and more, through either conversations with others or through my own discovery, I found that my knowledge base has moved from deep understanding to a broader, very surface level understanding of topics instead.

Practically, it looked more towards this:

X startup is brought up. I have heard of X startup, somewhere in an article or post, that I may or may not remember about. I may know what industry they are building in. If you asked me any detailed questions about what they really did, what their moat is, what are the risks or RTWs facing the industry they’re operating in, my mind goes blank. Any details I read about them originally went out the window.

Another example: Assume I read an article about compute scaling. It seems interesting, but ask me about it again a week later, and I can probably retrieve less than 5% of the info I learned, not kidding.

After a while, it became apparent to me it was actually very useless and inefficient the way I had been learning. Compared to college courses, with designed course structure, the bulk of all info I’ve absorbed within the last ~8 months can probably be crammed into 3 weeks of curriculum across my classes in a typical 10 week trimester back in university.

Recently, I have adopted a different learning system. It’s allowed me to take in much more sustained knowledge and reapply or retrieve it through time.

Below I will detail the 4 rules I have for learning that have allowed me to learn and retain knowledge with much more success. These are largely pulled from reflections from my college years and inspired from certain classes back then I learned a ton from.

Quick note: I am pro-AI (obviously), but for learning, I find there is a real negative impact it has on learning if done poorly. Below I also detail areas where AI can help, but I believe as a whole, the less you rely on AI for learning the better you learn.

Rules:

  1. Isolated, intense focus on whatever you are learning, no multitasking or distractions.

    I was a math and computer science double major back in college, but nowadays if we rank subject matter by total % of retention, economics is the subject I can pull the most knowledge from. Why is this? I only took 5 courses in economics in college.

    A deeper look into the course setup tells you. In CS courses, although I did well academically, professors at my school stuck by the standard cs principles and theorems, so YouTube videos can explain in half the time with clearer examples (no hate to my professors). In my mind, it made no sense to pay attention in class then, so often I zoned out plenty or multitasked on other items. I still attended every class and heard the big picture points. Econ was quite the opposite. Our professors all had their own distinct materials and nothing was ever posted online. The only way you could get access to notes from class was from your own handwritten notes copied from the whiteboard of a live lecture. 90% of grading also came from midterms and exams, meaning there was no room for distractions in class. Every class, I would take out a notebook, and listen closely for the entire 70 minute lecture and fill 2-3 pages of notes. Anything I did not understand was flagged on the columns and forced a trip to office hours to clarify. If I needed a restroom break I held it in. The result of that intense focus in class consistently through a term meant I came out with very high retention on what I learned in econ. To this day I continue to apply game theory learnings, and can explain the role of the Fed in a financial crisis.

    One thing to flag here is this type of uninterrupted deep focus is taxing. If I had 2 classes in the same day requiring intense focus, I would be too tired to even do homework after. For those with long work hours (myself included), I don’t recommend these deep focus sprints on work days, but only weekends. I still think they are extremely worth it, and you will probably get more out of a one hour uninterrupted focus sprint than a week of broad multi-tasked news reading or X scrolling.

  2. Immediate review followed by a refresh 1-3 days within learning.

    Immediate review in this case is note taking. Taking notes helps you retain information better, especially if you do it by hand. These days, I mostly pick out good writing or learnings and either type out manually the same content or paraphrase. I find doing it makes me remember content better than copying and pasting text over to my notepad and certainly better than solely reading without taking notes. Notetaking differs by person, some people prefer to write down every word, others bigger picture items. I usually don’t write a ton of content, preferring more to make sure I understand the content fully, and once I do, writing it in my own voice so I don’t forget it. I also use a loop process, which I will go in more detail in the subsequent rule, while notetaking that I find helpful.

    AI has negatively impacted this step. People use AI notetaking tools to get better quality notes and be more efficient in their learning. This hinders their learning. Using ai tools means you’re more removed from the process of learning, as a good deal of it comes from note-taking and understanding. You don’t need to understand a concept for AI to provide a summary, and you don’t have to write anything if AI already provides full notes. I strongly suggest against using AI at this stage. Spend the extra 10 mins to write down learnings in your own words and it will make your knowledge a lot less ephemeral.

    After this initial note taking pass, I’ll usually spend around 20 mins a day reviewing previous notes. During this time, it’s very important to make sure you maintain the understanding of the content. I have tested with different time periods and find the best is a quick review 1-3 days after, and a more deeper review a week after. After that, maybe one more review about a month or so out, and then I feel confident to retain pretty much 80% of the content in clear detail onwards.

    On the topic of deeper reviews, I find AI to be helpful in this case. You can put your notes into Claude and ask it to generate questions or considerations about the notes, so that you can actively practice your recall rather than passively taking in info again. Training this part of the process is very important for achieving longer term retention and the actual memory part of this.

    To make this work though, you need to have the initial note taking so you both a. actively do something with the info (process, restructure, and paraphrase) rather than passively read about it, and b. have a space to store the info to come back to again that is more efficient than rereading the entire article or rewatching the entire video.

  3. Organic reasoning and production devoid of excessive external influences.

    This is another step that is often skipped because of AI. Have you ever been overwhelmed by the length of an article or the depth of it, and asked AI for a summary of it but dummied down in an effort to understand it more easily. I am willing to bet you both were not able to really understand it that well either with the ai summary and also definitely were not able to remember the article details after a day or so.

    Organic reasoning and production is one of the most important ways for your brain to actually remember information. As I mentioned from above, one other alternative to traditional notetaking I have found very useful is a loop process: read the full article → move to a separate window → write out all you remember from the article → go back to the article to check what you missed and write the rest. This is great because it forces you not only to write from memory so you are tested on your memory instead of copying text down, but forcing you to practice writing with clarity and restructuring the content in your own words. (Try this yourself and lmk how it goes!)

    Beyond this, I also find writing essays on what you learn is a great way to go one step further. Back in school, professors pushed to go one step further, beyond just learning information but forming your own opinions and thoughts on these things. After doing this recently I have been pleasantly surprised by my ability to build off of ideas. Startups value first principles thinking, and you cannot have that reading AI summaries. Using AI to write also defeats the purpose here so this is an endangered skill. I either don’t use AI for writing at all now or for minimal editing after a draft is complete.

    Additionally, going through the process of writing a piece encourages maieutic learning, a form of socratic learning where you learn things from being prompted to think about what you already know and pulling from tacit knowledge. This rewires your brain to draw connections to different disciplines and form better connections to what you’re learning with what you already know. It’s a very underrated form of learning. (You can also use AI to act like a thought partner in this way, just make sure you’re the one thinking and answering, not them).

  4. Presentation of knowledge or teaching.

    The last part that has transformed my learning has been the presentation of knowledge or teaching. This can come through different mediums. The best way is through chats with others. Trading ideas is a great way because you not only learn new things from others but also practice your ability to retain information through explaining what you know to others. Teaching others is one of the best ways to understand something better yourself and test your true depth. Some of the most insightful findings about a subject matter was through an innocent, curious friend who asked questions or clarifications from our conversations. This in turn made me realize either something new I didn’t think of, and hardened the conceptual bridge between memory and retrieval even more.

    I guess this is why back in school we were made to do presentations on what we’ve learned. The deeper you learn and remember about a subject, the better you can explain or teach others about it, which in turn makes you understand that subject further, creating a flywheel that leads to expertise in that area.

I believe learning is a skill you can continue improving upon. These are my current findings with learning. I welcome any feedback and questions. If you have anything that has worked well for you, I’d also love to know!
